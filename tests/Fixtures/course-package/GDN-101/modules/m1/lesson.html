1. Why soil comes first

When a vegetable garden fails, people usually blame the seed, the weather or pests. Far more often the trouble is in the soil. Roots need three things from the ground they grow in: water, air and food. Soil that packs down hard gives them no air. Soil that drains in minutes gives them no water. Soil with nothing living in it gives them little food. Get the soil right and most plants look after themselves. Get it wrong and nothing you add later will fully make up for it.

This lesson covers what soil is made of and how to test your own, what pH means for your plants, how to make compost, and how to choose and fill containers. By the end of it you will know what your plants will be growing in, and how to make it good.

2. What soil is made of

A good garden soil is not just "dirt". By volume, a healthy loam is roughly half solid and half empty space:

What a good garden soil is made of, by volume
PartShareWhat it does
Mineral particles (sand, silt and clay)About 45%The body of the soil. Their sizes decide how it drains and holds water.
WaterAbout 25%Carries dissolved nutrients to the roots.
AirAbout 25%Roots and soil life need oxygen. Without it roots rot.
Organic matterAbout 5%Decayed plant and animal material. It holds water, feeds the soil life and binds particles into crumbs.

Those shares change all the time. After heavy rain the water share rises and the air share falls. In a dry spell the opposite happens. A good soil drains its extra water within hours, so the air comes back quickly.

Sand, silt and clay

The mineral part of soil is made of particles of three sizes. On the scale soil scientists use most often:

Sand
Particles from 0.05 mm to 2 mm across. You can feel each grain between your fingers. Sand drains fast and holds little water or food.
Silt
Particles from 0.002 mm to 0.05 mm. Silt feels smooth and floury when dry and slippery when wet.
Clay
Particles smaller than 0.002 mm. Clay feels sticky when wet and sets hard when dry. It holds water and nutrients well, but drains slowly and can shut out air.
Loam
A mixture of all three in which none dominates, roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay. Loam drains well but still holds water, which is why gardeners want it.

You cannot change your soil's particles, but you can change how they behave. Organic matter is the answer to both problems: it makes a sandy soil hold more water and opens up a clay soil so that water and air can move through it.

Testing your soil with a jar

You can measure your soil's texture with nothing more than a straight-sided glass jar. The particles settle out of water at different speeds: sand in a minute or two, silt over a few hours, and clay over a day or more.

  1. Dig down about 15 cm and take a cup of soil from the side of the hole. Pick out stones, roots and leaves.
  2. Fill the jar about one third with soil, then nearly to the top with water. Add a teaspoon of dishwashing liquid, which helps the particles separate.
  3. Close the jar and shake it hard for three minutes.
  4. Stand it where it will not be moved. After two minutes, mark the top of the bottom layer: that is the sand.
  5. After about four hours, mark the top of the next layer: the silt.
  6. Leave it for at least a day, until the water above is clearer. The last layer to settle is the clay. Floating bits on top are organic matter.
Worked example: reading the jar

The settled soil in Lerato's jar is 8 cm deep. The sand layer is 4 cm, the silt 2.5 cm and the clay 1.5 cm.

Sand: 4 ÷ 8 = 50%. Silt: 2.5 ÷ 8 = 31%. Clay: 1.5 ÷ 8 = 19%.

That is close to a loam, a little on the sandy side. It will drain well, and dry out faster than a pure loam in hot weather. Lerato's best move is to dig in compost every season, which will help it hold more water.

The "squeeze test" gives a quick first answer. Take a moist handful and squeeze it. Sandy soil falls apart when you open your hand. Clay soil holds a shiny, sticky lump you can roll into a sausage. Loam holds together loosely and crumbles when you poke it.

3. Soil pH

pH measures how acidic or alkaline something is, on a scale from 0 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral. Below 7 is acidic, above 7 is alkaline. Each step on the scale is ten times stronger than the one before, so a soil at pH 5 is ten times more acidic than one at pH 6.

pH matters because it controls which nutrients plants can take up. In a soil that is too acidic or too alkaline, the nutrients may be there but locked away from the roots. Most vegetables grow best between about pH 6.0 and 7.0. Potatoes cope with more acidic soil, and the cabbage family prefers the upper end of the range.

Soils in high-rainfall areas tend to be acidic, because rain slowly washes out calcium and other bases. Soils in dry areas tend to be neutral to alkaline. You cannot tell by looking: test it. Simple pH test kits and meters are sold at garden centres and cost far less than a failed season.

Changing your soil's pH
If your soil isWhat to addHow
Too acidic (below 6.0)Agricultural lime, or dolomitic lime, which also adds magnesiumFollow the amount on the bag for your area. Work it in a few weeks before planting, because it acts slowly.
Too alkaline (above 7.5)Compost and other organic matter, and in stubborn cases agricultural sulphurOrganic matter brings the pH towards neutral over seasons. Sulphur works faster but can overshoot: use only the amount on the label, and test again before adding more.

Never add lime "just in case". Liming a soil that is already neutral or alkaline pushes it out of the range vegetables like, and it is much harder to bring down again than it was to raise.

4. Compost

Compost is organic matter that soil organisms have broken down into a dark, crumbly material that smells like a forest floor. It is the single most useful thing you can add to any soil. It feeds the soil life, holds water in sand, opens up clay, and slowly releases nutrients. And you can make it for free from kitchen and garden waste.

Greens and browns

A compost heap needs two kinds of material, called greens and browns. The names describe what they do, not always their colour.

What goes into compost
Greens: rich in nitrogenBrowns: rich in carbonKeep out
Vegetable and fruit peelsDry leavesMeat, fish and bones
Fresh grass cuttingsStraw and dry grassDairy, and cooked food with oil
Coffee grounds and tea leavesShredded cardboard and newspaperDog and cat droppings
Green plant trimmingsSmall twigs and wood chipsDiseased plants, and weeds in seed

A good working mix is roughly two to three parts browns to one part greens, by volume. Too many greens and the heap turns slimy and smells of ammonia. Too many browns and it sits for months doing nothing. Meat, dairy and oily food attract rats and flies, and pet droppings can carry parasites, so they stay out.

Making a heap or a bin

  1. Choose a spot on bare soil, in part shade, out of the wind. In a small space, use a closed compost bin with a lid.
  2. Start with a layer of coarse browns, such as twigs, so air can get in from below.
  3. Add greens and browns in layers, or mix them as you go, keeping to about two or three buckets of browns for each bucket of greens.
  4. Keep it as damp as a wrung-out sponge. Water it in dry weather, and cover it in heavy rain.
  5. Turn it with a fork every week or two, moving the outside to the middle. Turning lets air in and speeds everything up.

A well-built heap of about a cubic metre heats up within days, reaching 55 to 65 °C in its centre. That heat kills most weed seeds and disease organisms. Small bins rarely get as hot, so keep weeds in seed out of them. Compost is ready in two to six months, when it is dark, crumbly and smells earthy, and you can no longer recognise what went in.

No room for a heap? A worm farm turns kitchen scraps into rich worm castings in a box under the sink or on a balcony. It uses composting worms, such as red wigglers, not the earthworms you dig up in the garden.

A case from practice: Thandi's smelly bin

Thandi started a compost bin on her balcony with a month's vegetable peels and grass cuttings from a neighbour. Two weeks later it was wet, slimy and smelled of ammonia, and fruit flies swarmed when she lifted the lid.

What went wrong: her bin held only greens. With no browns to balance the nitrogen, and no air in the wet mass, the wrong organisms took over. She mixed in two buckets of shredded cardboard and dry leaves, turned it, and left the lid slightly open for a few days. Within a week the smell was gone.

5. Containers

In a small space, containers let you grow food on paving, on a balcony or against a sunny wall. Almost anything can be a container, as long as it is big enough, drains, and is safe for food.

Size

The commonest mistake is a pot that is too small. A small pot dries out in hours on a hot day, and the roots run out of room. As a guide:

The smallest container for common crops
CropDepthVolume
Herbs, lettuce, spring onions15 to 20 cm5 litres or more per plant
Swiss chard (spinach), beetroot20 to 25 cm10 litres or more per plant
Carrots30 cm, deeper than the variety's rootsA box or trough
Tomatoes, brinjals, peppers30 cm or more20 litres or more per plant
Worked example: how much potting soil to buy

Sipho has a balcony box 100 cm long, 25 cm wide and 25 cm deep. He will fill it to 3 cm below the rim, so the soil is 22 cm deep.

Volume: 100 × 25 × 22 = 55,000 cm³. There are 1,000 cm³ in a litre, so that is 55 litres.

Potting soil in South Africa is often sold in 30 dm³ bags. A cubic decimetre is a litre, so each bag holds 30 litres. Sipho needs two bags, 60 litres, which leaves a little over for topping up after the soil settles.

Drainage

Every container needs holes in its base so that extra water can escape. Without them, water collects at the bottom, the roots sit in it, and they rot for lack of air. If a container has no holes, drill several before you fill it. Stand pots on small blocks or pot feet so the holes are not sealed against the floor, and use saucers only where water would damage the surface below, emptying them after watering.

What to fill them with

Do not fill containers with garden soil. In a pot it packs down into a dense mass with no air, drains badly, and may carry weed seeds and pests. Use a good potting soil, which is made to stay light and open in a container. Mixing in up to a quarter of your own compost feeds it and helps it hold water.

Old paint tins, chemical drums and treated timber can leach harmful substances into the soil. Use food-safe plastic, terracotta, untreated wood or fabric grow bags for anything you will eat.

6. Mulch and water

Mulch is a layer of material spread on the soil's surface: straw, dry leaves, wood chips or compost. It keeps the soil cool, slows evaporation, stops rain from crusting the surface, and smothers weeds. As it breaks down it adds organic matter. Use a layer of 5 to 7 cm in beds and 2 to 3 cm in containers, kept a few centimetres away from plant stems so they do not rot.

Water deeply and less often, rather than a little every day. A deep watering sends roots down where the soil stays moist. Push a finger into the soil: if it is dry 2 to 3 cm down, water. Water in the early morning, so leaves dry during the day and less water is lost to heat. Water a container until water runs from its drainage holes, so the whole root ball is wet.

Containers dry out much faster than beds. In a summer heatwave a small pot may need water every day, and a large one every two or three days. In much of South Africa water is scarce and restrictions are common, so mulch and deep, well-timed watering are not extras. They decide whether a small-space garden survives a dry spell. Collecting rainwater from a gutter in a covered drum is one of the best investments a small garden can make.
